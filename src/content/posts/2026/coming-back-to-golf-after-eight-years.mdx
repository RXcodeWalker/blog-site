When I lived in the U.S. during first and second grade, golf was just a normal part of my week. I used to go to proper golf courses and take regular classes, learning the basics before I even realized how rare that kind of access actually is. Then we moved back to India, and for the next eight years, I didn't touch a club once. It wasn't that I stopped liking the sport, I just never had the chance to play, and eventually golf faded into one of those childhood memories you assume you have moved on from.

But honestly, moving into my new house and having a golf simulator right in the building changed that overnight. For the past two months, I have finally had a way to pick the game back up, and stepping onto the mat again after eight years felt both strangely familiar and completely brand new at the same time.

At first, I thought getting back into rhythm would mostly be a physical process of shaking off eight years of rust and hitting enough balls until my swing came back. And in fairness, putting in consistent reps over these two months has made a massive difference to my ball-striking. My irons feel much cleaner off the face than they did in those first few awkward weeks, and even my driver, which used to feel unpredictable every time I teed it up, has started finding a far more dependable groove. Seeing every shot's data appear on the projector screen right after impact gives you a quiet, honest look at how much your swing is actually evolving from week to week.

However, the deeper I get into these sessions, the more I realize that golf isn't really testing my mechanics as much as it is testing my focus. Whenever I walk into the simulator room with a clear head and commit fully to each shot, the ball comes off the club cleanly and the numbers back it up. The moment I let my mind wander toward schoolwork, rush through a session because I am short on time, or carry whatever stress I had from the rest of the day onto the mat, my swing falls apart almost immediately. Thin strikes, lazy tempo, and wild misses all trace back to the exact same thing... my attention drifting in the second before impact.

What makes that realization so interesting is how little room golf leaves for going through the motions. In most parts of daily life, you can operate on autopilot for twenty minutes and still get decent results, whereas a golf ball responds solely to the quality of your presence at the moment you swing. Stepping into that space downstairs has quietly become a mirror for where my head is at on any given evening, forcing me to slow down and actually be in the room before I even take the club back.

Of course, coming back to a sport after nearly a decade away means the progress is never going to move in a straight line. I still have sessions where my timing feels effortless and everything flies straight down the virtual fairway, followed the very next day by a session where nothing clicks and the dispersion looks rough. Earlier, having a bad day on the mat would have frustrated me, especially when I felt like I had just figured something out the day before. Now, I take those swings in stride because good days and bad days are simply part of learning any craft properly, and after waiting eight years just to play again, even an off session feels like a privilege.

To wrap things up, having this simulator in my building feels like a second chance at a game I thought I had left behind in second grade. I still have a long way to go before my swing feels truly natural, and plenty of frustrating afternoons ahead of me as I keep working on it. As long as I bring my full focus every time I step onto the mat, though, I know I'm moving in the right direction.
